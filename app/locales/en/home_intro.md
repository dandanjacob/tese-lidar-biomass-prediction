Aboveground biomass is the central variable for quantifying forest carbon stocks — roughly half of biomass is carbon — and the Amazon is one of the largest terrestrial reservoirs of this element. Estimating AGB accurately is what makes it possible to measure how much carbon a forest sequesters or releases, the basis for national emission inventories, mechanisms such as REDD+, carbon credits, and the monitoring of climate targets (Paris Agreement). Without reliable, spatially distributed estimates, it is impossible to distinguish intact from degraded forest, to size losses from deforestation, or to validate global carbon-cycle models — hence the value of cross-referencing field inventory (the "ground truth" measured tree by tree) with sensors that can extrapolate that measurement over large areas.

The main bottleneck in AGB estimation is that it depends heavily on the vertical structure of the forest — tree height, density, and canopy architecture — precisely the dimension that optical sensors (Landsat, Sentinel) see poorly, since they only "see" the top of the canopy and saturate in dense forests like the Amazon. LiDAR emits laser pulses that penetrate the canopy and return a 3D profile of the vegetation, directly measuring height and structure — the variables with the greatest predictive power for biomass. This makes it far more accurate than spectral indices, especially in high-biomass tropical forest, and allows calibrating allometric relationships (LiDAR metrics ↔ field AGB) that can then be extended across space and time, reducing the dependence on field inventories that are costly and logistically unfeasible at Amazonian scale.

This project estimates Amazon biomass by cross-referencing LiDAR data (NASA/ORNL) with field forest inventories. The pipeline processes the raw data, finds intersections between plots and LiDAR campaigns, clips the point clouds for each plot, extracts relevant metrics, and models biomass using different allometric formulas.
